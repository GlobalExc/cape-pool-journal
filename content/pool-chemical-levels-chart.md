faqs:
- q: Which pool chemical should I adjust first?
  a: Total alkalinity first, then pH, then chlorine. Alkalinity buffers pH, so fixing pH first often means it drifts straight back. Chlorine works best once the pH is in range.
- q: How often should I test pool water?
  a: Test pH and chlorine 2–3 times a week in summer and weekly in winter. Test alkalinity, calcium hardness and stabiliser about once a month, or after heavy rain or a large top-up.
- q: What does ppm mean?
  a: 'Parts per million: milligrams of the chemical in each litre of water. 1 ppm of chlorine is 1 mg per litre.'
- q: Why does my pool test fine but still look dull?
  a: Check the filter and pump hours. Balanced water still needs filtering. Also check calcium hardness and total dissolved solids. Water that is very high in these can look flat, and a clarifier may help.

## The chart

| Reading | Ideal range | Too low causes | Too high causes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| pH | 7.2–7.6 | Stinging eyes, corrosion of metal fittings, etched marbelite | Weak chlorine, cloudy water, scale |
| Free chlorine | 1–3 ppm | Algae, bacteria, green water | Irritated eyes and skin, faded swimwear |
| Total alkalinity | 80–120 ppm | pH that swings up and down | pH that's hard to lower, cloudy water |
| Calcium hardness | 200–400 ppm | Surface etching, rough grout | Scale on tiles and in the salt cell |
| Stabiliser (CYA) | 30–50 ppm | Chlorine burns off in sunlight within hours | Chlorine becomes sluggish ("chlorine lock") |
| Salt (salt pools) | Per manual, often 3,000–4,000 ppm | Chlorinator shows low-salt warning, low output | Salty taste, corrosion risk |

## pH: the master setting

pH measures how acidic or basic the water is. The scale runs from 0 to 14, and 7 is neutral. Pools sit just above neutral, at 7.2–7.6, which is close to the pH of human eyes.

- **To lower pH:** add pool acid (hydrochloric) or dry acid (sodium bisulphate), diluted and poured in front of a return with the pump running.
- **To raise pH:** add soda ash (sodium carbonate).

Salt chlorinators push the pH up over time, so salt pools usually need acid every week.

## Free chlorine: the sanitiser

Free chlorine is the chlorine that is still available to kill germs and algae. Keep it at 1–3 ppm. Keep it near the top of that range in hot weather or after a lot of swimming.

If the *combined* chlorine (total minus free) is above about 0.5 ppm, that's the strong "pool smell" people mistake for too much chlorine. It actually means you need *more*: a shock dose clears it.

## Total alkalinity: the shock absorber

Alkalinity stops the pH jumping around. Use sodium bicarbonate (bicarb) to raise it. Pool acid lowers it slowly, over several small doses.

## Calcium hardness: protecting the surface

Water that's low in calcium pulls calcium out of plaster, marbelite and grout. Calcium hardness increaser (calcium chloride) raises it. Lowering it usually means partly draining and refilling.

## Stabiliser: sunscreen for chlorine

Cyanuric acid protects chlorine from UV. Without it, strong summer sun can burn off most of your chlorine within a few hours. Don't overdo it: stabilised chlorine tablets add CYA every time, so test it monthly.

## In Cape Town: two local quirks

- **Soft water.** Cape Town's municipal water is relatively soft. Low calcium hardness is common, especially in newer pools and after a big refill. Test calcium at the start of every season.
- **Winter rain dilution.** Heavy rain drops every reading at once. After a big front, retest before adding anything.

## Safe chemical handling

- Add chemicals to water, never water to chemicals.
- Never mix chlorine and acid, even in a bucket. This releases chlorine gas.
- Store chemicals dry, shaded, separately and away from children.
- Wear gloves and eye protection when handling acid.
