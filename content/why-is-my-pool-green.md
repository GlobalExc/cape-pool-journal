- q: Is it safe to swim in a green pool?
  a: No. Green water means the chlorine isn't controlling the pool, so bacteria may be growing alongside the algae. Murky water also hides the floor, which is a drowning risk, especially for children. Wait until the water is clear and the free chlorine is back to 1–3 ppm.
- q: Why did my pool turn green after rain?
  a: Rain dilutes the chlorine and usually lowers alkalinity. It also washes in leaves, soil and pollen that feed algae. After heavy Cape Town rain, test the water and add chlorine within a day or two, before algae takes hold.

## The short version: algae, and why it won

Green water is algae. Algae spores are always blowing into your pool. They only take over when the sanitiser can't keep up. In practice, one or more of these went wrong:

- **Free chlorine dropped below about 1 ppm.** Sun, rain, swimmers and debris all use it up.
- **The pH crept above 7.8.** At high pH, chlorine is much weaker, even when the test shows "enough".
- **Filtration fell short.** Short pump hours, a dirty filter or a blocked basket means water isn't being cleaned.
- **Debris built up.** Leaves and dirt on the floor feed algae and use up chlorine.

## Day 1: Prepare the water

1. **Get the debris out.** Net out leaves and empty the skimmer and pump baskets.
2. **Test the water.** Bring the pH into the 7.2–7.6 range and the alkalinity to 80–120 ppm *before* shocking.
3. **Brush everything.** Walls, floor, steps and behind the ladder. Algae clings to surfaces, and brushing lets the chlorine reach it.
4. **Shock in the evening.** Add a heavy chlorine dose for your pool's volume. Follow the product label: a green pool usually needs a double or triple "normal" shock dose. Add it in the evening so the sun doesn't burn it off.
5. **Run the pump 24 hours.**

## Day 2: Filter and repeat

By morning the water should be turning from green to cloudy blue or grey. That's the dead algae.

- Backwash the filter and rinse it. The filter is now catching dead algae and will clog fast.
- Retest. If free chlorine has dropped below about 3 ppm, shock again that evening.
- Brush again.

## Day 3: Clear and balance

- Backwash again. If the water is still hazy, a clarifier or flocculant helps the filter catch fine particles.
- Vacuum dead algae off the floor. If you flocculated, vacuum *to waste*, not through the filter.
- Once the water is clear and the free chlorine is down to 1–3 ppm, rebalance and swim.

## How to stop it coming back

| Habit | How often |
| --- | --- |
| Test pH and chlorine | 2–3 times a week in summer |
| Empty baskets and skim | Every few days, or daily in windy weather |
| Brush walls and floor | Weekly |
| Backwash the filter | Weekly, or when the pressure rises 10 kPa above clean |
| Pump run time | 8–10 hours a day in summer |
| Shock | Weekly in summer, and after heavy rain, a heatwave or a party |

## In Cape Town: when green pools happen

Cape Town pools most often turn green at three times of year:

- **Spring (Sept–Oct):** The water warms up after a diluted, under-filtered winter.
- **After the first summer heatwave:** Chlorine burns off quickly, and the pool isn't keeping up.
- **After big winter storms:** Rain dilution and debris arrive together.

If you know a heatwave or a cold front is coming, test and top up chlorine the day before.

## When to call a professional

Call a professional if you can't see the floor at all, if the pool has been green for weeks, if there are mosquito larvae, or if it's still green after three days of the steps above. The filter or pump may be the real problem.
